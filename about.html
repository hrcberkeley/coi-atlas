Investigations Lab

About the atlas

The atlas helps lawyers find what has been written about conditions in a country. It shows who wrote it, when, and about which place. It does not judge whether a report is right or whether a place is safe.

Who made it

Guillén Torres made the atlas at the Investigations Lab. It was built in collaboration with AI: Claude, a model made by Anthropic, wrote most of the code and ran most of the data checks. Guillén Torres designed the atlas, decided what it should and should not do, and reviewed the work at every step.

Where the documents come from

All documents come from ecoi.net, a public collection of country of origin information. We use a selection of about 83,000 documents, not the whole collection. For each document we keep what ecoi.net records about it: the institution that published it, the date, the language, the type of document and its description.

How the text was prepared

We copied the text out of every page of every PDF. Where a page was a scanned image, the text was read by text-recognition software (Apple Vision). The text was then split into passages, which are usually paragraphs. Each passage keeps the page it came from, so the atlas can show it on the printed page.

Some lines are not part of what a document says: page numbers, notices, disclaimers, tables of contents, reference lists and photo credits. Fixed rules find these lines and leave them out of every search, count and list. You can show them again under the grid. A sample check found that about one in eight visible passages is still such a line.

Documents that are not in English were translated into English by machine (the Opus-MT models, and NLLB for other languages). The translation is shown under the original. It can be wrong; the original is always the text that counts.

Places

Place names are found by matching the text against a list of known places (GeoNames). Provinces and their borders come from geoBoundaries. A place is only found if it is spelled the way the list spells it, so some mentions are missed. A sample check found that about 7 in 100 place tags were wrong.

Searching

Exact words finds passages that contain your words as whole words. When results are sorted by best match, passages are ranked with a standard formula (BM25) that favours passages that use your words often, in fewer words. Very short lines, such as headings, are put after full passages. Ranking changes only the order, never which passages count.

Similar wording finds passages that say something close to your phrase, even in other words or another language. It uses a language model (multilingual-e5-small) that turns each passage into a list of numbers; passages with close numbers have close meanings. The model runs in your browser, and your phrase is never sent anywhere. It works best when the phrase is in the language of the documents you want to find.

Same wording elsewhere lists passages in other documents that are almost the same text, such as a quote, a later edition or a translation.

Topics

Topics come from the questions that decision-makers asked in query responses, such as "treatment of journalists". Similar questions were grouped into topics automatically, and each topic is named after one of its own questions; no model writes the names. A passage joins a topic only when it is very close to that topic's questions. A sample check found that about 7 in 10 filings are right, so a topic is a starting point, not a complete list.

Checking the data

We checked samples of the data with a mix of automatic tests and AI reviewers, with a second reviewer on every doubtful case. The automatic tests check, for every country, that the counts add up and that the files agree with each other. The figures quoted on this page come from those checks.

Your privacy

The atlas is a set of files that runs in your browser. Your searches, evidence and notes stay on your computer.
