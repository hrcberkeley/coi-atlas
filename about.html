Investigations Lab

About the atlas

Lawyers use the atlas to find out what was written about a country. It shows who wrote it, when, and about which place. It does not say whether a report is right. It does not say whether a place is safe. That is the lawyer's job.

If you are new, follow one case through the atlas. It takes twenty steps and shows every screen.

Who made it

The Investigations Lab made the atlas. People at the Lab decided what it should do and what it must never do. They checked the work at every step. Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, wrote most of the code and ran most of the checks on the data.

Where the documents come from

Every document comes from ecoi.net. Ecoi.net is a public library of reports on the countries people flee. The atlas holds about 83,000 of its documents. That is a selection, not the whole library. For each document we keep what ecoi.net says about it. That means who published it, when, in what language, what kind of document it is, and how ecoi.net describes it.

Some reports cover a whole region or the whole world. Ecoi.net files such a report under every country it names, and so does the atlas. A small country can therefore show passages from a long report that mentions it once. When a report names 10 or more countries, the atlas says so on the card. It also hides those reports when you open a topic. One click brings them back. Before you cite a passage, make sure it is about your country.

How the text was prepared

We copied the text out of every page of every PDF. Some pages were only pictures of paper. Apple Vision read those. Then we cut the text into passages. A passage is usually a paragraph. Each one remembers its page, so the atlas can show it where it was printed.

Some lines say nothing about the country. Page numbers do not. Neither do disclaimers, tables of contents, reference lists or photo credits. Fixed rules find these lines and leave them out of every search, count and list. You can bring them back under the grid. In a sample check, about one visible passage in eight was still such a line.

Reports in other languages were translated into English by machine. We used the Opus-MT models, and NLLB for the rest. The translation sits under the original. It can be wrong. The original is the text that counts.

Places

The atlas finds a place when the text spells it the way GeoNames spells it. GeoNames is a public list of the world's places. Province borders come from geoBoundaries. A village spelled another way is missed. In a sample check, about 7 place tags in 100 were wrong.

Searching

Exact words finds passages that hold your words as whole words. When you sort by best match, a standard formula called BM25 puts first the passages that use your words often and in few words. Headings and other short lines go after full passages. The order changes. The count does not.

Similar wording finds passages that say something close to your phrase. They may use other words, or another language. A language model called multilingual-e5-small turns each passage into a list of numbers. Passages whose numbers are close mean close things. The model runs in your browser. Your phrase is never sent anywhere. It works best when you write in the language of the reports you want.

Same wording elsewhere lists other documents that hold almost the same text. It might be a quotation, a later edition or a translation.

Looking across countries

The first page can look for words or a topic in every country at once. The map and the list show how many of each country's documents hold the words, or speak to the topic. They show the years, too. These are counts of documents, not of passages.

The counts come from an index of every word in the collection. We built it in advance. Your browser fetches only the small pieces it needs, and your words never leave your computer. A phrase in quotes counts a document only where the words stand side by side. Words without quotes count a document that holds all of them anywhere, so that number can run high. Word endings like activist* are checked only inside a country.

Reports that cover 10 or more countries are left out unless you ask for them. Big collections have more of everything. The share of a country's own documents is often the fairer number.

Similar wording works across countries too. For this we squeezed the meaning of every passage into a small code. Your browser turns your phrase into numbers and fetches only the codes near it, a few megabytes. It returns the 2,000 passages it finds closest, in any language, nearest first. Then it counts their documents country by country. Squeezing loses a little. In a test, the passages it found were very nearly as close as the true nearest ones. Reference lists and repeated text are not in it.

Topics

The topics come from questions. Asylum officials sent questions to research units, such as how journalists are treated in a certain city. A computer grouped similar questions together. Each topic is named after one of its own questions.

A passage joins a topic in one of two ways. Most join because the report put them under a heading that matches the topic. The rest have no such heading. A model read their words and filed them. The atlas marks those passages, and you can hide them with one tick.

Here is how the models learned. A large model called Qwen 2.5 read 57,000 sample passages. For each one it named the topic. It also said whether the passage was reference material, such as a citation, a source list, a table of contents or a list of names. Small models learned from those answers and then scored every passage in the atlas. Reference material stays out of topic lists. It stays in searches. Each topic list shows its clearest passages first.

A second model checked a sample. About 9 passages in 10 that it could judge were under the right topic. Nearly every passage left out really was reference material. Still, about 3 passages in 10 with real content belong to no topic, and every list has a few mistakes. Treat a topic as a place to start.

Checking the data

We checked samples by machine and by AI reviewers. A second reviewer looked at every doubtful case. For every country, tests make sure the counts add up and the files agree with each other. The numbers on this page come from those checks.

Your privacy

The atlas is a set of files that runs in your browser. Your searches stay on your computer. So do your evidence and your notes.
